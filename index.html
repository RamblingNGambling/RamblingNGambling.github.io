    How to read it

    Put a price on each side. Type a stake on either side and the other side fills in with the amount that balances the pair, so both outcomes return the same money.

    Type over either stake at any time and the other one follows. You can also type a payout to work backwards to the stake that reaches it.

    Prices read as American (+150, −130) or decimal (2.50).

    What arbitrage is

    Two books can price the same market differently. When the two prices disagree enough, you can back both sides and win the same amount whichever way the game goes. This is an arbitrage, or an arb.

    The calculator adds the implied chance of each price. If the total is below 100%, an arb exists. It then divides your stake so that both sides return the same money.

    How to use it

    Put the best price you can get on each side into the two fields. Put the money you want to risk in total into the stake field. The tool gives you the amount for each side and the profit.

    The split is what makes it work. Equal stakes on both sides do not lock the return.

    What to watch

    Prices move. Get the second side down quickly, or you hold one side alone. Books also limit or close accounts that only take arbs. Check that both bets are on the same market, the same line and the same period.

    How to read it

    Leave the hedge stake empty and the tool fills in the amount that makes both outcomes pay the same. That amount is the equal hedge, and the locked figure is your profit whichever way the game goes.

    Type your own hedge stake to take less than the full lock. The two outcome figures then differ, and the locked figure shows the worse one.

    Add the book’s cash-out offer to see which is better. The offer is the money returned to you, stake included.

    When to hedge

    A hedge turns an open position into a settled one. You give up the top end and you remove the risk. It is the right move when the remaining edge is small next to the money at stake — a live futures ticket, the last leg of a parlay, a big price that has shortened a long way.

    It is the wrong move when you still hold the edge. If the current price on the other side is worse than fair, a full hedge sells your position at a loss to the market.

    Cash out is a hedge with a fee

    A book’s cash-out is the same trade, priced by the book. It is almost always worse than hedging by hand, because the book takes its margin on both the original price and the current one. Put the offer in the field and the tool tells you the gap in money.

    Partial hedges

    You do not have to lock the whole thing. Put in half the equal hedge and you keep half the upside and remove half the risk. The two outcome figures show exactly where that leaves you.

    What a round robin does

    A round robin is not one bet. It is every parlay of a chosen size that can be made from your legs. Four legs by 2s is six separate two-leg parlays. Each one is priced and settled on its own.

    The point is that you no longer need every leg. With four legs by 2s, two winners already pay one bet back. The cost is that you risk six stakes instead of one, so a clean sweep pays less than the straight four-leg parlay would.

    How to size it

    The stake here is per bet, which is how books take it. Decide the total you want to risk first, then divide by the number of bets. Six bets and a 0.3u budget is 0.05u per bet.

    Reading the table

    Each row is a number of winning legs. Where your legs carry different prices, which legs win changes the return, so the row shows the range from worst to best. Profit is that return less the whole ticket.

    Find the row where profit first turns positive. That is how many legs you need.

    How to read it

    The top half turns a chance into the price that pays fairly for it. The bottom half turns a price into the chance the book is charging you for.

    The bottom figure still has the vig in it. To take the vig out you need both sides of the market — use the devig tool.

    Trading on Kalshi, Novig or Polymarket? Those markets quote in cents and percent, not American prices. The cheat sheet lists every percent from 1 to 99, in whole steps or in halves, with its American and decimal price. Open the cheat sheet →

    Why the two directions matter

    Every price is a percentage in disguise. −110 is 52.38%. +150 is 40.00%. If you think a side wins more often than the price says, the bet has value.

    How to use it

    Start with your own number. If you think a team wins 57% of the time, put 57 in the top field. The tool gives you −133. Any price better than −133 is a bet worth making by your own estimate.

    Go the other way to check a price. Put −250 in the second field and you get 71.43%. That is how often the bet must win to break even.

    Percent markets

    Exchanges price in percent or in cents. Kalshi and Polymarket quote a contract from 1¢ to 99¢, which is the chance in percent. Novig shows a percentage beside every price. This tool moves between that world and a sportsbook price, so you can compare an exchange with a book on the same scale.

    For a full table, see the cheat sheet.

    Remember the vig

    The percentages from a book add up to more than 100%. That extra is the book’s margin. A single price is never a fair estimate on its own.

    How to read it

    Enter both sides of the market. Each row gives the fair chance and the fair price for that side, with the book’s margin taken out. The two percentages add up to 100%.

    Compare a fair price with what another book offers. A better price than the fair one is a positive edge.

    What the vig is

    A book prices both sides so that the two chances add up to more than 100%. On a −110 / −110 market the two sides come to 104.55%. The extra 4.55% is the hold. It is how the book makes money.

    To devig is to remove that extra and leave two numbers that add up to 100%.

    The three methods

    Multiplicative divides each side by the total. It is the standard method and the right first choice.

    Additive takes an equal share of the margin off each side. It gives the favourite a lower fair chance than the multiplicative method does.

    Power raises both chances to the same power until they add up to 100%. It moves longshots the most, so it is the one to reach for on big prices.

    The three agree closely on an even market. They separate as the market gets one-sided. On a −1000 / +700 market they differ by more than two points.

    How to use it

    Take both sides of the market from the sharpest book you have. Put your side in the first field. Read the fair line, then look for a better price elsewhere. That gap is your edge.

    How to read it

    Find your chance in the left column of a block. The price beside it is the one that breaks even at that chance. A price better than that one is a bet with value.

    Under 50% the price is a plus number. You risk one unit to win more than one. Over 50% the price is a minus number. You risk more than one unit to win one. At 50% the price is +100, an even bet.
