    What an arb is

    Two books can price the same market differently. When they disagree enough you can back both sides and win either way. If the two implied chances add up to under 100%, an arb exists.

    The split is what makes it work. Equal stakes on both sides do not lock the return.

    What to watch

    Prices move, so get the second side down fast or you hold one side alone. Books limit accounts that only take arbs. Check both bets are the same market, line and period.

    How to read it

    Leave the hedge stake empty and the tool fills in the amount that pays the same either way. The locked figure is your profit whichever side wins.

    Type your own stake to lock less and keep some upside.

    Add the cash-out offer — money returned, stake included — to compare.

    When to hedge

    Hedging trades the top end for certainty. It is right when the edge left is small next to the money at stake — a live futures ticket, the last leg of a parlay.

    It is wrong when you still hold the edge. At a price worse than fair, a full hedge sells your position at a loss.

    Cash out is a hedge with a fee

    The book’s cash-out is the same trade with its margin on both prices, so hedging by hand almost always beats it. Put the offer in the field to see the gap in money.

    How to read it

    A straight parlay is one bet. Every leg must win.

    A round robin splits the legs into every smaller parlay of the size you pick. Four legs by 2s is six bets, and the stake is per bet.

    The table shows the return for each number of winners, as a range where legs are priced differently.

    Why a round robin

    You no longer need every leg. Four by 2s pays a bet back on two winners. The cost is six stakes instead of one, so a clean sweep pays less than the straight parlay would.

    How to size it

    The stake is per bet, which is how books take it. Decide your total first, then divide by the number of bets. Six bets on 0.3u is 0.05u each.

    Reading the table

    Each row is a number of winning legs, with the range from worst to best where the legs differ. Find the row where profit first turns positive. That is how many legs you need.

    The Kalshi case
    
    
    A Kalshi ticket shows a multiplier and no American price. Put 3.33 in the multiplier field and
    you get +233 and 30.03%, which is what you need to compare it against a book.
    
    
    The multiplier is the whole payout, not the profit. 3.33× on $10 returns $33.30 and
    profits $23.30.
    
    
    When the legs do not reach the payout
    
    
    On a combo ticket the printed leg multipliers often do not multiply to the payout. They are
    display values, not always the prices the ticket was built from. The cost and the payout are what
    the book is bound by, so trust the ticket line over the leg line.
    
    
    Fees
    
    
    A fee is paid on top of the stake and does not come back, so it comes off the profit. Put it in
    and the line under the figures gives your true price.

    What the vig is
    A −110 / −110 market adds up to 104.55%. That extra 4.55% is the hold. To devig is to remove it and leave two numbers that add to 100%.
    The three methods
    Multiplicative divides each side by the total. The standard, and the right first choice.
    Additive takes an equal share off each side, giving the favourite a lower fair chance.
    Power raises both chances to the same power until they reach 100%. It moves longshots most, so reach for it on big prices.
    The three agree on an even market and separate as it gets one-sided. On −1000 / +700 they differ by more than two points.
